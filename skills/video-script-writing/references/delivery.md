# Writing for a flat delivery

Some narrators are monotone, or simply do not want to perform. This is not a
defect to hide. It is a constraint that changes what the words should do, the same
way a fixed lens changes what you shoot.

The rule underneath all of it: **nothing in the script may depend on the voice
doing something it will not do.**

## What breaks in a flat read

| Written | What happens when read flat |
|---|---|
| "This is *insanely* fast" | sounds sarcastic, or like the narrator doesn't believe it |
| "You won't *believe* what happened next" | sounds like a threat, or a stall |
| "It's not a tool — it's a *workflow*" | the contrast vanishes; nothing lands |
| "So… are you ready?" | rising intonation missing; sounds like an accusation |
| three adjectives in a row ("fast, clean, reliable") | three stresses to place, none placed |
| a 32-word sentence that changes subject halfway | the listener loses the thread; the problem is the thread, not the length |

## What works instead

**Facts with a number.** "Forty minutes, start to finish." The number is the
emphasis, and it does not need a voice.

**Understatement.** "It asked for a password it printed three lines earlier."
Delivered evenly, that is dry humour. Delivered loudly, it is a complaint.

**Sentences carry a connected thought.** The flat voice does not need short
lines; it needs sentences that are complete and clearly built. Let a sentence run
to the end of its thought, and split it only when it stumbles or loses the thread.
A fragment is not a short sentence; it reads as a slide, and it loses the verb
that made the thought a thought. The full rule is in
`../../voice-dna/references/continuous-prose.md`.

**The admission before the correction.** "I said the port was right. It wasn't."
Two beats, no emphasis required, and the turn does all the work.

**Concrete nouns where a hype word would go.** "One laptop" not "an incredible
setup". "Same file" not "a seamless experience".

**Let the cut be the punchline.** End the sentence before the joke and cut to the
next shot. The edit supplies the timing the voice doesn't.

## Rewrites

**Before** (needs performance):
> And honestly? That is absolutely insane. In just under forty minutes, we went
> from a completely broken setup to a fully working media stack — and you can too!

**After** (works flat):
> It went from a broken stack to a working one in forty minutes, on the same
> laptop.

**Before:**
> So what did I learn? Well, the biggest lesson was that Docker permissions are
> more important than you'd think.

**After:**
> Docker wasn't running. My user wasn't in the group. That was the whole problem.

**Before:**
> Let's dive into the settings, where things get really interesting.

**After:**
> Open the settings page, where three fields matter.

## Practical rules

1. **A sentence can carry a claim and its cause, condition or consequence.**
   Split it only when the reader would lose the thread, not to keep every
   sentence short. A fragment is never a sentence.
2. **No exclamation marks.** None. They are instructions the voice won't follow.
3. **No hype adjectives.** exciting, amazing, insane, crazy, incredible, awesome,
   unbelievable. Delete the adjective; keep the fact.
4. **No rhetorical questions.** They need intonation to be questions.
5. **No "not X, but Y".** The emphasis lands on the wrong half when read flat.
6. **Test each line:** read it in the most bored voice you can manage. If the
   meaning survives, the line is fine. If it needs a lift, rewrite it.
7. **Put the energy in the edit** — stingers, cuts, on-screen numbers, speed ramps.
   That budget is separate from the voice and should be spent deliberately.

## Delivery notes for the narrator

When handing the script over, say these once, at the top:

- Read at your normal pace. Do not try to sound excited.
- Pauses after full stops are welcome; they are cut points, not dead air.
- If a line feels wrong in the mouth, stop and say so — the line gets rewritten,
  not forced.
- Mistakes: pause, repeat the sentence from the top. No need to restart the take.

A narrator who is told not to perform gives a better read than one who is told to
sound enthusiastic. The second one is acting badly; the first one is just talking.

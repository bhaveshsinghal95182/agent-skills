# Video hooks and intros

Two different jobs, often confused:

- **The cold open** is the first 3–10 seconds. It buys attention with a picture.
- **The intro** is the next 20–40 seconds. It converts attention into a reason to
  stay: hook, invalidate, roadmap.

A greeting at the top destroys both. It asks the viewer to wait for the interesting
part, and the interesting part is the only thing that has ever held anyone.

Every line here is a complete sentence. A hook can be one sentence or two, and
its length follows the thought; it cannot be a fragment. Related facts join into
one sentence instead of a fragment per line. The shared rule is in
`../voice-dna/references/continuous-prose.md`.

## The intro formula

Three beats, in this order, every time:

**1. Hook** — the variable opening. One specific moment, number, or claim. No
setup, no context, no channel business.

**2. Invalidate** — name the default belief or advice this video destroys, in one
or two sentences. This is what makes the video feel necessary rather than nice.

**3. Roadmap** — 2–3 payoffs, written as full spoken clauses:
"First, the part that broke. Then the setting that fixed it."
Not clipped fragments ("- the fix"). A fragment reads as a slide; a clause reads
as a person.

Hook + invalidate should run 50–80 words. The roadmap is 2–3 lines. Longer than
that and it becomes a table of contents.

## Hook variants

Pick one. Across a batch, never use the same variant twice in a row.

**A. Personal-failure confession.** Common belief → what you did → how it failed →
what it cost → what you found instead. Strongest default, because the failure is
proof you actually did the thing.

**B. Viewer-tension callout.** "If you've got X and it keeps doing Y…" → the exact
failure they have already hit → the advice that didn't help → tease the real
answer.

**C. Bold reframe.** A claim about something the viewer thinks is working → the
assumption underneath it → the tension → the hidden side.

**D. Specificity gap.** Name a familiar tool → the specific result it gives → the
observation that punctures it → roadmap.

**E. Mid-effort scene.** Drop the viewer into a specific frustrating moment
already in progress → invalidate the fatalistic reading → roadmap.

## Hooks for a flat or monotone voice

If the voice is not expressive, the hook has to work on **content alone**. That
rules some variants in and some out:

**Work well** — A (confession), E (mid-effort scene), D (specificity gap). All
three are carried by what happened, not by how it was said. Delivered flat, they
read as honesty.

**Work against you** — C (bold reframe) and anything built on a rhetorical
question, because a flat read turns a bold claim into a shrug and a question into
a stall.

Rules that follow from a flat delivery:

- **No hype words in the hook.** "Exciting", "insane", "crazy" delivered evenly
  read as sarcasm or disappointment. Cut the word, keep the fact.
- **No rhetorical questions.** They need rising intonation to work.
- **No contrastive stress.** "It's not a *tool*, it's a *workflow*" needs emphasis
  the voice won't give. Assert the second half.
- **Let the failure carry the emotion.** "It crashed on the fourth reboot" is
  dramatic on its own; you do not have to sell it.
- **Say less than feels right.** A flat voice plus understatement is deadpan; a
  flat voice plus a sentence that explains itself twice is a lecture.

## The energy handoff

When the voice cannot supply energy, something else has to. The cleanest structure
is a handoff, not a ramp:

```
0:00–0:06   cold open — the result on screen, one dry line, no greeting
0:06–0:14   hard cut to a stinger (music, title card, date/number)
0:14–0:28   promise + invalidate
0:28–0:40   roadmap, then straight into the first beat
```

The stinger is doing the work a "hey guys, welcome back!" does in a high-energy
channel: it marks the start, sets the tone, and gives the viewer a beat to settle
before the explanation begins. Because it is not the voice, it can be loud.

Rules for the handoff:

- **Hard cut, not a fade.** A fade says "this is a montage". A cut says "here we
  go".
- **The stinger lands on picture, not on black.** Title over the working result,
  not over a logo.
- **One stinger per video**, at the top. Reusing it mid-video cheapens the opening.
- **The first line after the stinger must be content**, not a greeting — the
  stinger has already done the greeting.

## The promise line

Somewhere in the first 30 seconds, in one sentence, say what this is. A noun
phrase, matching the title:

> This is a media stack that runs on one laptop, and I'll show you the whole setup.

One sentence. Same claim as the title and thumbnail. If the promise drifts
mid-video, retention breaks at the drift, not at the start.

## Retention after the hook

- **Open loops, then close them.** Never more than two open at once.
- **Announce the length.** "Three things went wrong" gives the viewer a budget to
  spend. A vague middle feels endless.
- **Deliver the opening frame's promise inside the first third**, not only at the
  end.
- **Cut every sentence whose only job is to move you between topics.** That is
  what a cut is for.

## Testing before shooting

Read the hook to someone who has not seen the topic. Ask:

1. What is this video about?
2. What will you get if you watch?
3. What happens next?

Any "I don't know" means the hook is unfinished. Correct answers delivered with a
shrug mean the problem is the picture, not the words.

For a flat delivery, add a fourth: **would this still work if read in the most
boring voice you can imagine?** If not, the line is leaning on performance that
isn't there.

## Deliverable

1. The cold open as shot beats: what is on screen, and the one line over it
2. The stinger/transition point, if the channel uses one
3. Hook + invalidate + roadmap, in full sentences
4. One sentence on why this variant fits the topic

Under 120 words of narration total. A hook that needs 200 words is a hook made of
setup.

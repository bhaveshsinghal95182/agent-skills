# The bullet script

Word-for-word scripts come out stiff on camera. The stiffness lives in the joins
between ideas — the "so, moving on" sentences a written draft needs and a person
never says. Bullets remove the joins and let the narrator supply the phrasing.

This format is better for flat or monotone speakers than a full script, because
the narrator is **reconstructing** the sentence rather than **performing** it.
Reconstruction sounds natural even in a bored voice; recitation does not.

Only the opening line is word-for-word. Everything else is scannable bullets.

The bullets are scaffolding for the narrator, not a licence for fragments in the
finished delivery. When a bullet becomes a spoken sentence it keeps its subject
and verb, and two bullets about the same decision usually become one sentence.
The full prose rule is in `../../voice-dna/references/continuous-prose.md`.

## Output shape

```
[SAY VERBATIM] <one sentence — the first line of the video>

## INTRO (bullets, 4 beats)
- Pain/tells: <the specific symptoms the viewer recognises>
- Credibility + stakes: <who you are, what it was costing you — one line each>
- Pivot: <what you did instead>
- Preview: <2–3 teasers for the biggest payoffs>

## POINT 1: <short name>

**Hook:** <one sentence>
**Beat 1 — the insight**
- <the claim the hook set up, stated plainly>
**Beat 2 — proof**
- <specific fact, number, or walkthrough>
**Beat 3 — example**
- <what actually happened on screen>
*(On-screen: <what to show>)*

## CTA (bullets)
- Problem: <the pain the CTA targets>
- Solution: <what the link is>
- Benefit: <the specific outcome>
- Where: <"link in the description">

## END SCREEN (bullets)
- What was just taught
- The bridge to the next video
- What they'll see if they watch it
```

## Rules

**Hook options while drafting.** Give 2–3 per point, labelled **DR** (desired
result) and **P** (pain). Once the creator picks one, the final version keeps only
that line.

**Beat counts.** Three or four beats for a normal point. Five or more only when
each beat is a genuine step of a framework. More than four on a normal point means
the point is repeating itself.

**Beat 1 is the insight.** State the claim; don't bury it under setup.

**Every beat after that does unique work.** If two beats make the same claim from
different angles, merge them. Four distinct points beat six repetitive ones.

**Bullets must be specific.** Each bullet tells the narrator *what to say*, not
what to talk about.

- Bad: "Talk about the memory problem."
- Good: "Corrections don't stick between chats. Your taste lives in the finished
  script. The AI only ever sees the prompt."

**If a bullet runs past two lines, split it.** It has to be readable at a glance.

**Add on-screen cues** where the shot changes from talking head to screen:

```
- I said the port was right. It wasn't.
*(On-screen: settings page, port field showing 8080)*
```

**No fake examples.** Never invent a case study or a client. If the source doesn't
supply the example, cut the beat and lean on the insight instead.

**CTAs and end screens are always bullets**, never word-for-word. The narrator
freestyles the transition; the four labelled beats keep it on target.

## What this format is not

- Not an excuse to skip structure. The bullets still follow hook → proof →
  example, and the points still follow the outline.
- Not a script for someone who wants to read. If the narrator prefers a full read,
  write the full script instead and use `references/delivery.md`.
- Not shorter to write. Each bullet takes more thought than a sentence, because it
  has to leave the narrator something real to say.

## When the take comes back

Bullet scripts produce three failure modes worth checking:

1. **Filler explosion** — the narrator fills the gaps with "um, so, basically".
   Fix: fewer bullets per point, more specific ones.
2. **Lost structure** — they riff past a beat. Fix: number the beats and keep the
   hook line visible on screen while recording.
3. **Drift into hype** — without a script, people reach for "this is crazy". Fix:
   put the two or three phrases that must not be said at the top of the page.
